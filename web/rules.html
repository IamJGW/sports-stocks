The idea

Every listed NFL quarterback, running back, wide receiver and tight end (120 of them) is a stock with a short ticker, like Josh Allen. You start with $100,000 of play money. Buy shares of the athletes you think will play well. When they play, you're paid a dividend for every share you hold, and their share price moves with how they're playing.

Nobody owns an athlete outright: any number of players can hold the same star. It's play money only, just for bragging rights.

Buying and selling

You trade with the market maker, a computer trader run by the game. It's always there with two prices for each athlete:

  • Buy price: what you pay for a share.
  • Sell price: what you get for a share.

The two are 4% apart. For an athlete worth $50.00, you'd buy at about $51.00 and sell at about $49.00. So buying and selling straight back always loses a little. The game is for picking athletes who will do well, not for flipping shares.

The more people own, the higher the price

Every $5,000 worth of an athlete that players hold pushes his price up 1%. Selling brings it back down. Each share in a big order is priced one at a time, so a big order costs more per share than a small one. The game shows you the total, the average price and his price after your trade before you confirm.

There's no limit on how much of one athlete you can own, but it gets expensive. Put all $100,000 into one $50.00 athlete and you get 1,791 shares, not 2,000, at an average of $55.82. His price ends up at $59.75. Sell it all straight back and you'd get $96,060.

No trading during his game

Once an athlete's game kicks off, the market maker stops trading him until the overnight update. Otherwise you could watch a three-touchdown first half and buy at yesterday's price.

After each game, the athlete's stats are turned into fantasy points (half-point-per-catch scoring):

Then the points are turned into money:

Dividend per share = fantasy points × $0.1136
You're paid = dividend per share × shares you held at the start of game day

You own 100 shares of a quarterback. He throws for 300 yards, 3 touchdowns and 1 interception, and runs for 20 yards.

The fine print

  • Who gets paid: whoever held the shares at midnight Eastern at the start of game day. Buying on Sunday morning doesn't get you Sunday's games; buying on Saturday night does. Selling on Sunday morning still gets you Sunday's dividend.
  • Bad games: a game with negative points pays nothing. It never costs you money.
  • Playoffs: playoff games pay 1.5× the normal dividend.
  • When: dividends land in your cash in the overnight update after the game.
  • Stat corrections: the NFL sometimes fixes stats days later. The game pays once, on the box score at the time, and doesn't take money back or top it up.

Why $0.1136? It's set so the average listed athlete, at an average price of $50.00, pays about 2% of his price in dividends each week of the season ($1.00 a share a week). Dividends alone won't make you rich; picking the athletes who beat expectations will.

Why prices move

Each athlete has a fair value: what his dividends are expected to be worth. The market maker's prices sit around it.

Fair value = expected weekly dividend ÷ 2%

Where the starting price comes from

Last season's points per game, adjusted for two things that tend to happen the next year: stars cool off a bit and weaker players catch up a bit (lean toward the group average), and athletes miss games and fall back (repeat factor). Each position group has its own:

GroupLean to averageRepeat factor
QB7%66%
RB30%77%
WR/TE38%69%

Example, Josh Allen: 21.45 points a game last season, leaned to 21.03, times the 66% repeat factor gives 13.88 expected points a game. Over 17 games at $0.1136 a point and 18 weeks, that's $1.49 a week, and $1.49 ÷ 2% = $74.47.

Every night: this season counts more and more

After each game day, the expected points blend last season's estimate with what he has actually scored this season. Last season starts out worth 4 games. After 4 games this season counts the same, and by week 12 it counts three times as much. A game his team played without him counts as zero, so an injury pulls his price down on its own.

Every week: re-checking each group

After the Monday night game, the game checks each position group against what it has actually paid this season. If wide receivers as a whole are paying 15% more than promised, their prices rise a little. A slow group gets cheaper.

  • Early on, one hot week barely counts: the old estimate is worth 8 weeks of evidence.
  • It moves at most 5% a week, and stays between 80% and 120% of the original estimate.
  • The market report shows each week's re-check.

And what players do

On top of fair value, buying pushes an athlete's price up and selling pushes it down (see Buying and selling).

Net worth: how you're scored

Net worth = cash + what selling all your shares would bring right now

Your shares are valued at what the market maker would actually pay for all of them, at the sell price and including the drop a big sale causes. So buying a pile of one athlete to push his price up never makes you look richer than you are.

A day in the game

  • During the day: trade whenever you like, except athletes whose game has started.
  • Overnight: the game reads the box scores, checks that they add up, pays dividends, updates every fair value, and writes the market report: biggest dividends, biggest price moves, and how your portfolio did. If a box score looks wrong, nothing is paid until it's fixed.
